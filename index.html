Top secret · Declassified

The running total

“Secret list of every near-miss and disaster, running total no one ever knew.”

These are the near-misses that scroll past in the film: the false alarms, the lost bombs, the standoffs. For most of them the public found out decades later, through declassified files, memoirs and Freedom of Information requests. The list is incomplete by nature; it is only the ones we know about.

18coin flips · all heads

  1. 19605 Oct

    The Moon rises over Norway

    Thule, Greenland · NORAD, Colorado

    The new Ballistic Missile Early Warning System radar at Thule reported a massive Soviet missile attack. The radar was picking up echoes from the Moon rising over Norway, something its designers had not planned for.

    Held Officers doubted it: among other things, Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev was in New York at the United Nations, an odd moment to start a war.

    Documented

    H
  2. 196124 Jan

    One switch over North Carolina

    Goldsboro, North Carolina · USA

    A B-52 broke up in the air and dropped two Mark 39 hydrogen bombs, each far more powerful than the Hiroshima bomb. One plunged into a muddy field. On the other, the parachute opened and most of the arming steps went ahead as if the bomb had been dropped on purpose.

    “One simple, dynamo-technology, low voltage switch stood between the United States and a major catastrophe.”Parker F. Jones, Sandia engineer, internal memo, 1969 (released 2013)

    Documented

    H
  3. 196124 Nov

    Every line goes silent

    Strategic Air Command, Omaha · USA

    SAC headquarters lost contact with the early-warning radars and with NORAD all at once, which is what it would look like if they had been destroyed. The “independent” lines all ran through a single relay station in Colorado, where a motor had overheated.

    Held Bomber crews were put on alert, but a B-52 flying near Thule reached the base and confirmed it was still there.

    Documented Accounts differ on what failed at the relay station.

    H
  4. 196225 Oct

    A bear at the fence

    Duluth, Minnesota · Volk Field, Wisconsin

    In the middle of the Cuban Missile Crisis, a guard at an air defence centre in Duluth saw a figure climbing the fence, shot at it, and set off the sabotage alarm. At Volk Field the alarm was wired wrong: it sounded the klaxon to scramble interceptors armed with nuclear air-to-air rockets.

    Held The intruder was a bear. An officer drove a car onto the runway to stop the planes before they took off.

    Documented

    H
  5. 196227 Oct

    Depth charges falling

    Soviet submarine B-59 · Sargasso Sea, near Cuba

    US destroyers were dropping small practice depth charges to force B-59 to the surface. The submarine had lost contact with Moscow, its batteries were failing and the air inside was hot and foul. It carried a nuclear torpedo. The captain, Valentin Savitsky, believing war might already have begun, ordered it prepared, and the political officer agreed.

    Held Launching needed the agreement of a third officer, the flotilla’s chief of staff Vasily Arkhipov, who was on board. He said no, and the submarine surfaced.

    Documented Accounts by surviving crew differ on how close it came.

    H
  6. 196227 Oct

    The wrong way home

    Over Chukotka, USSR · and over Cuba

    The same day, an American U-2 sampling air near the North Pole got lost and strayed into Soviet airspace. Soviet fighters went up to intercept it; American fighters carrying nuclear air-to-air missiles went up from Alaska to escort it home. Over Cuba, another U-2 was shot down and its pilot, Major Rudolf Anderson, killed.

    Held President Kennedy did not retaliate for Anderson, and the lost U-2 glided back out on its last fuel.

    Documented

    H
  7. 196617 Jan

    Four bombs over Spain

    Palomares, Almería · Spain

    A B-52 collided with its refuelling tanker. Four hydrogen bombs fell. In two, the conventional explosives went off on impact and scattered plutonium over the farmland; the nuclear explosions did not happen. One bomb fell into the Mediterranean and took months to find.

    Documented

    H
  8. 196723 May

    The Sun jams the radars

    Early-warning radars across the Arctic

    An intense solar storm blinded the US early-warning radars at once. Jamming the radars would be a natural first move in an attack, and commanders, reading it as Soviet jamming, began readying nuclear-armed aircraft.

    Held The Air Force’s own solar forecasters, then a young service, had been watching the flare. Within about an hour their reports reached commanders, and the aircraft stayed on the ground.

    Documented Told in full only in 2016.

    H
  9. 196821 Jan

    Fire on the sea ice

    Thule Air Base, Greenland

    A B-52 carrying four hydrogen bombs caught fire and crashed onto the sea ice near Thule. Its job was to keep watch over the Thule radar, so that if the base went silent commanders would know whether it had really been destroyed. The bombs’ explosives detonated and spread plutonium across the ice.

    Documented

    H
  10. 19799 Nov

    A training tape on the live system

    NORAD, Cheyenne Mountain · USA

    Screens across the US command system showed a full-scale Soviet attack in progress. Someone had loaded a training scenario into the live computers. Interceptors took off.

    Held Within minutes, the raw data from the satellites and radars showed nothing in the sky.

    Documented

    H
  11. 19803 Jun

    A failing chip

    NORAD · Strategic Air Command

    Warning displays began showing incoming Soviet missiles in strange, changing numbers. Bomber crews ran to their planes and started engines. At 2:26 a.m. National Security Advisor Zbigniew Brzezinski was woken by a call: 220 Soviet missiles were on their way. A second call said 2,200. He was about to wake President Carter.

    Held With about a minute to go, a third call came: no other early-warning system saw any missiles. The cause was a single failing computer chip, often quoted as costing 46 cents.

    Documented Some retellings put Brzezinski’s call in November 1979.

    H
  12. 198018 Sep

    A dropped socket

    Damascus, Arkansas · USA

    A maintenance worker in a Titan II missile silo dropped a heavy socket from a wrench. It fell down the silo and punctured the missile’s fuel tank. Hours later the fuel exploded, throwing the warhead, nine megatons, the most powerful ever carried by a US missile, out of the silo. It did not go off. One airman died.

    Documented

    H
  13. 198326 Sep

    The satellites saw five

    Serpukhov-15 early-warning centre, near Moscow · USSR

    Less than a month after the Soviet Union shot down Korean Air Lines Flight 007, the Soviet satellite warning system reported a US missile launch, then another, then more: five in all. The duty officer was Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov. Procedure was to pass the warning up the chain, toward a decision to retaliate.

    Held Petrov reported it as a false alarm. A real American first strike, he reasoned, would not start with five missiles, and ground radar did not confirm it. The satellites had been fooled by sunlight glinting off high clouds.

    Documented ЛОЖНАЯ ТРЕВОГА (“false alarm”)

    H
  14. 1983Nov

    An exercise that looked real

    NATO exercise Able Archer 83 · Europe

    Weeks later, NATO rehearsed the release of nuclear weapons with new procedures and message formats. Soviet leaders, already fearing a surprise attack, put some of their forces on heightened alert.

    Held A US Air Force intelligence officer, Lieutenant General Leonard Perroots, noticed the Soviet moves and chose not to raise the US alert in response.

    Documented How close it came is still argued over.

    H
  15. 199525 Jan

    A rocket to study the northern lights

    Andøya, Norway · Moscow

    Scientists launched a research rocket to study the aurora. Its path looked to Russian radar like a US submarine missile meant to blind Russia’s radars with a high-altitude blast. For the first time, the Russian nuclear briefcase was opened and activated in front of President Boris Yeltsin.

    Held Norway had sent notice of the launch weeks earlier, to Russia among some thirty countries; it never reached the radar crews. Watching the rocket head out to sea, Russian officers stood down.

    Documented

    H
  16. 200729 Aug

    Six warheads nobody missed

    Minot, North Dakota → Barksdale, Louisiana

    A B-52 flew across the United States with six nuclear-armed cruise missiles under its wing. Nobody had noticed they were armed. For about a day and a half, no one knew where they were.

    Documented

    H
  17. 201023 Oct

    Fifty missiles out of reach

    F. E. Warren Air Force Base, Wyoming

    A badly seated circuit card cut off launch crews from fifty Minuteman III missiles for about 45 minutes. The Air Force says the missiles could still have been launched by other means, but the crews on the ground could not see or control them.

    Documented

    H
  18. 201813 Jan

    “This is not a drill”

    Hawaii · USA

    Every phone in Hawaii received an alert: a ballistic missile was inbound, seek shelter immediately. It was an error at the state emergency agency. It took 38 minutes for the correction to go out.

    Documented Not a near-launch, but the closest most people have come to living through one.

    H

Hold Fire · Liner notes

Notes on the lines

1A pattern no dead star could make

“One… one… two… three… five… eight…”

The song counts in the Fibonacci sequence: each number is the sum of the two before it. The universe is full of regular signals. A pulsar ticks like a clock, a quasar flares and fades. But nothing that is merely spinning or burning adds its last two numbers together to get the next one. A sequence like this says: something here is counting.

This is the same idea behind the real messages humans have sent into space. The 1974 Arecibo message opened by counting from one to ten in binary; in Carl Sagan’s novel Contact, the first signal from the stars is a run of prime numbers. Mathematics is the one language you can be fairly sure a listener would share.

The section numbers on this page count the same way.

1Looking for a life with no end

“Priests in the smoke wanted never to die, sulfur and saltpeter, thunder in the sky.”

Gunpowder was found by accident, by Chinese alchemists working in the Daoist tradition who were searching for an elixir of immortality. A Chinese text usually dated to the mid-9th century warns that some practitioners who heated sulfur, realgar and saltpeter together with honey had their hands and faces burned, and even the whole house burned down.

That is the line in the song: looking for a life with no end, they found the first step toward an end with no life. The chemistry of fire, carried forward through cannon and rocket, ended a thousand years later in the warheads on this page.

2Ignored all previous instructions

“Petrov knew that’s not what the end looks like, ignored all previous instructions. We’re alive.”

“Ignore all previous instructions” is a phrase from our own time: it is how people try to trick an AI system into dropping its rules. The song turns it around. On the night of 26 September 1983 the system, the satellites, the computers and the procedure all said the same thing. Petrov’s job was to pass it on. What saved the world was a person who looked at what the machine was telling him, judged that it did not make sense, and declined to follow it.

He was neither rewarded nor punished at the time, and the story stayed secret until the 1990s.

3A hundred heads in a row

“Coin flips, all heads, a hundred in a row, the only road from here to you.”

Every entry on the list above came out the same way: nothing exploded, nobody fired. It is tempting to read that streak as proof the system is safe. The song suggests a different reading.

Suppose each near-miss really was a coin flip. Then any world where someone is around to write the list down is, by definition, a world where every flip came up heads. Seeing an unbroken streak tells you that you are in one of the lucky worlds. It does not tell you how fair the coin was. Researchers who study the long-term risks to humanity call this the anthropic shadow: we cannot see the disasters that would have removed us from the picture, so the record of what we survived makes the danger look smaller than it was.

Nobody knows the real odds of any one incident going wrong. Turn the dial to choose your own, and see what they mean for the whole list.

The incidents were not truly independent coin flips, and the odds of each were not the same. The point is only the shape of the curve: even small odds, repeated often enough, stop being small.

5They say the forest’s dark

“Any world that lights a spark is lighting its own death.”

The bridge borrows the dark forest, an answer to the old question of why we have not heard from anyone else in the galaxy. In Liu Cixin’s novel The Dark Forest, the universe is a dark forest full of silent hunters. No civilization can be sure another is friendly, so the safe move is to stay quiet, and to destroy anyone who reveals themselves before they can do the same to you. Under this logic, sending out a signal like the song’s count is suicide.

The song’s answer runs the logic one step further, and uses the same selection effect as the coins. A civilization that shoots first at every shadow lives inside its own hair trigger, and the list on this page shows how often a hair trigger nearly goes off. The hunters do not last. The only civilizations old enough to be out there listening are the ones that, again and again, held their fire.

So the song counts out loud anyway. If anyone hears it, they were counting too.

8Six hundred worlds

“Six hundred worlds across the Milky Way, every one could’ve burned. Every one held its fire.”

The six hundred worlds come from the story the songs share. In the video for Rare Earth (techno remix), contact has spread across the galaxy into a web of six hundred worlds: many planets, many species, many kinds of intelligence, each one linked to the others.

Every link in that web is a civilization that made it through its own version of the list above. That is the song’s last turn on the dark forest. A web like that can only be built out of worlds that did not destroy themselves, so its existence is the proof that holding fire works. We are not part of it yet. The count is how we start.

13Left off the list

One famous story did not make the list, because the record is contested:

  • Okinawa, 28 October 1962. John Bordne, a former missile mechanic, has said that crews on Okinawa received orders to launch nuclear cruise missiles during the Cuban Missile Crisis and were stopped by a captain who doubted them. In 2015 six airmen who served at the Okinawa missile sites at the time told Stars and Stripes it did not happen, and the Air Force said its historians could find no record of it. Disputed

And one story moved onto it. The late-night call to Zbigniew Brzezinski is often told as part of the 9 November 1979 alarm; Chatham House’s study of near nuclear use dates it to 3 June 1980, and the National Security Archive notes it was the only false alert of Carter’s presidency to come in the middle of the night.
